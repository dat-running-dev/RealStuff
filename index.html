Chairs, briefly.

Most people sit in a chair for hours each week without thinking about it. This page covers where chairs came from, the main kinds, what their parts are called, and how to check whether one fits you.

A short history

  • Ancient Egypt, around 3000 BCE

    Stone and wooden thrones were for pharaohs and senior officials. Sitting was a sign of rank, and the shape of a seat told you who was in charge.

  • Ancient Greece and Rome

    The klismos, a light chair with curved legs, appears throughout Greek art. Romans used folding stools, the sella curulis, which magistrates carried on campaign.

  • Medieval Europe

    Most people sat on benches or stools. The tall, high-backed chair stayed a symbol of authority, usually placed at the head of the table for the lord of the house.

  • Renaissance to Baroque

    Upholstery, carved detail and turned legs moved into ordinary homes, and chairs began to be sold as matching sets.

  • The industrial age

    Michael Thonet's bentwood chairs, made in large numbers from the 1850s, were cheap and light enough to ship worldwide.

  • Modern design

    Steel, plywood and molded plastic let designers such as Marcel Breuer, Charles and Ray Eames and Arne Jacobsen make chairs that are still sold today.

Kinds of chair

  • Dining chair

    Upright, with a seat height that suits a table. Built for sitting through a meal, not for lounging.

  • Armchair

    Padded arms and a deep seat. Good for reading and for long conversations.

  • Office chair

    Adjustable height, tilt and lumbar support, for people who sit at a desk most of the day.

  • Rocking chair

    Curved runners let it sway. Often used for nursing, and for evenings on a porch.

  • Lounge chair

    Reclined and well padded, for stretching out. The mid-century versions are still popular.

  • Stool

    No back and no arms. Small and flexible, for counters, workbenches and quick seats.

Parts of a chair

  1. Crest rail: the top of the backrest. Often the most decorated part.
  2. Back posts: the uprights that hold up the backrest.
  3. Seat: the surface you sit on. Its height and depth matter most for comfort.
  4. Stretchers: the rails between the legs that stop the chair racking.
  5. Legs: they carry the weight and mostly set the chair's style.
  6. Arms: optional supports that help you sit down and stand up.

Seat height: most adult seats sit between 40 and 46 cm (16 to 18 in) off the floor. Of everything on this page, seat height has the biggest effect on comfort.

Checking a chair

  1. Height

    Sit back and let your feet rest flat. Your knees should be about level with your hips. If your thighs slope down, the seat is too low; if your feet dangle, it is too high.

  2. Depth

    Leave two or three finger-widths between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knee.

  3. Back

    The backrest should follow the curve of your lower back. If you will sit for long periods, adjustable lumbar support is worth paying for.

  4. Purpose

    Think about what you will do in it: quick meals, long reading or a full working day. Those answers change which chair is right.
