A seat for every purpose

The humble chair has been shaping how we live for five thousand years.

Chairs signal status, support posture, and set the tone of a room. This guide walks through where they came from, the main kinds you will meet, and how to pick one you will actually enjoy sitting in.

Explore the types

A brief history

Ancient Egypt, c. 3000 BCE

Carved stone and wooden thrones were reserved for pharaohs and officials. Sitting was a sign of rank, and the seat's shape often showed who was in charge.

Ancient Greece and Rome

The klismos, a graceful chair with curved legs, appeared in Greek art. Romans added folding stools (the sella curulis) that were used by magistrates and could be carried on campaign.

Medieval Europe

Most people sat on benches or stools. The heavy, high-backed chair was still largely a status object, often kept near the head of the table for the lord of the house.

Renaissance to Baroque

Upholstery, carved detail, and turned legs spread through European homes. Chairs became part of the furniture set rather than a single throne.

The industrial age

Bentwood chairs, mass-produced by Michael Thonet in the 1850s, made good seating cheap and light enough to ship around the world.

Modernism and beyond

Designers such as Marcel Breuer, Charles and Ray Eames, and Arne Jacobsen used steel, plywood, and molded plastic to create chairs that are still in production today.

Common types of chairs

Dining chair

Upright and sturdy, with a moderate seat height. Designed for long meals rather than lounging.

Armchair

Padded arms and a deep seat for relaxing. A favorite in living rooms and reading corners.

Office chair

Adjustable height, tilt, and lumbar support for people who sit for hours at a desk.

Rocking chair

Curved runners let the chair sway gently. Popular for nursing and for slow evenings on a porch.

Lounge chair

Reclined and cushioned, built for stretching out. The mid-century lounge chair is a classic example.

Stool

No back and no arms. Compact and flexible, good for counters, workshops, and quick seating.

Anatomy of a chair

  1. Crest rail: the top of the backrest, often the most decorative part.
  2. Back posts: the uprights that carry the backrest.
  3. Seat: the surface you sit on. Its depth and height affect comfort most.
  4. Stretchers: the rails between legs that add rigidity.
  5. Legs: they carry the load and set the chair's overall style.
  6. Arms: optional supports that help you stand up and rest your elbows.

Did you know?

A chair's seat is usually 40 to 46 cm (16 to 18 in) high, which suits most adults. Seat height is the single biggest factor in whether a chair feels comfortable.

How to choose a chair

Check the height

Your feet should rest flat on the floor with knees at about a right angle. If they dangle or your thighs slope upward, the seat is the wrong height.

Test the depth

Leave two to three finger-widths between the seat edge and the back of your knee so the chair does not press on your legs.

Support your back

A good backrest supports the natural curve of your lower spine. For long sitting, look for lumbar support you can adjust.

Match the room

Think about how you will use the chair: quick meals, long reading sessions, or a desk you use all day. The right answer changes with each.
